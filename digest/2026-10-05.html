Apple tightens macOS Full Disk Access after AI agent risks surface

The week’s signal is clear: AI agents are no longer toy experiments — they’re running on your actual machine, and the platforms are reacting. Here’s what happened this week and what it means for anyone shipping autonomous systems.

1. Apple Tightens macOS ‘Full Disk Access’ for AI Agents

Apple announced tighter controls on macOS Full Disk Access permissions, explicitly citing new risks from AI agents. This follows weeks of community reporting where developer-mode AI coding tools were scraping sensitive system files at scale.

Heed angle: This is exactly the class of risk Heed addresses. When an AI agent gets elevated filesystem access, you need a governance layer that can intercept tool calls before they hit the OS boundary — not after. Our plugin-enforced approach maps naturally here: a before_tool_call hook on any file-system operation could enforce least-privilege policies. The question isn’t whether we’ll see platform-level agent restrictions; it’s whether those defaults will be good enough. They won’t be. That’s why teams need their own policy layer sitting in front of every agent invocation.

2. OpenAI Safety Employee Resigns, Claims “Culture Is Broken”

A safety researcher at OpenAI has resigned publicly, claiming the company’s safety culture is broken. WSJ previously reported that OpenAI cut ties with three safety researchers as well. The resignations highlight growing internal tension between deployment velocity and safety oversight.

Heed angle: Internal safety culture matters, but it’s fragile. When engineers change, when priorities shift, policy enforcement needs to survive the organizational churn. Plugin-enforced governance doesn’t rely on individual commitment — it’s structural. Every tool call goes through the classifier, regardless of who’s typing. If OpenAI’s safety departures tell us anything, it’s that human-centric safety nets have leakage points. Automated, auditable gates don’t quit.

3. Google Freezes Open Source Bug Bounty Over AI Slop Submissions

Google paused its open-source Vulnerability Rewards Program after a “significant rise” in AI-generated submissions, most of which were invalid. Linux saw a similar problem. The pause lasts until at least next year.

Heed angle: The same pattern applies bidirectionally: if unauthenticated AI tools can flood a bug bounty program with junk, then uncontrolled agents operating in production can absolutely do far more damage — exfiltrating data, misconfiguring infrastructure, or modifying critical code. An interception layer that validates intent before action execution is the anti-slopproficiency measure for real operations.

4. Show HN: Inkog — Pre-flight Checks for AI Agents

A new project surfaced on Hacker News: Inkog, offering pre-flight checks for AI agent governance, loop detection, and injection prevention. It’s joining a growing category of tools focused on agent safety hooks and behavioral guardrails.

Heed angle: The category is validating itself. Multiple projects now target pre-execution validation for agents — loop detection, injection guards, permission checkpoints. Heed is positioned at the intersection: a unified policy engine that covers all these concerns with a single classification step (before_tool_call) rather than scattering logic across dedicated hook modules. The differentiation is composability — one classifier replaces a toolkit. 5. Sam Altman: “The World Should Accept Some Bad Things Happening”

In a Politico interview, Altman said accepting “bad things” as collateral for AI benefits distinguishes OpenAI from Anthropic’s approach. The comment drew immediate pushback from safety advocates and policymakers.

Heed angle: Voluntary acceptance of harm is not a governance strategy. If the leading AI companies are openly debating whether acceptable-risk thresholds should be implicit rather than enforced, the case for explicit, runtime-enforced governance only grows stronger. Heed exists to answer the question everyone in this conversation seems to be avoiding: “Who checks the checkers?” The answer isn’t a CEO’s statement. It’s a rule that fires before the dangerous call executes.

Bottom Line

Three themes converge this week:

  1. Platforms are hardening around agent access (Apple, Google)
  2. Internal safety cultures are fracturing (OpenAI)
  3. The governance tool category is maturing (Inkog, and others like ourselves)

For teams shipping AI agents — especially those with real tool access — this is the moment to treat governance as infrastructure, not an afterthought.
