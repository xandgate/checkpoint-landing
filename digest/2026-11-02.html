The plugin-enforced governance layer for AI agents.

This Week: Enforcement Is Becoming Infrastructure

Six months after a single Cursor agent wiped a production database in nine seconds, the industry has moved past “should we govern?” to “what architecture enforces governance without killing developer velocity?” Three distinct approaches surfaced recently — static intent graphs, pre-flight scanning, and local safety nets — each revealing a different blind spot in the traditional HITL model. Together they confirm what Heed built from day one: persuasion (system prompts, moral suasion) doesn’t work at production scale. Only infrastructure-layer enforcement does.

1. Kintsugi: Local-First Safety Nets for Human+Agent Teams

Show HN: Kintsugi – A local-first safety net for AI agents and humans

Kintsugi takes a fundamentally different approach than proxy-based or MCP-hook governance. Instead of sitting between the agent and its tools, it wraps both — giving humans a first-class interface to observe, intervene, and veto agent actions without changing any code. The name references the Japanese art of repairing broken pottery with gold: embrace the fragility, make it visible, build strength through repair. Practically, it means an engineer can step in during execution without instrumenting every tool call in advance — exactly the kind of emergent-control capability that breaks down in complex multi-agent workflows.

Heed angle: Kintsugi solves the visibility problem; Heed solves the enforcement problem. They’re orthogonal. When combined, you get both real-time human awareness (Kintsugi’s dashboard overlay) and guaranteed policy enforcement (Heed’s Mode 1–4 architecture). For teams running agents against production systems where visibility matters as much as control, pairing them eliminates the single point of failure inherent in either approach alone.

2. ExploitGym: Demonstrating That AI Agents Can Launch Real Attacks

ExploitGym – Can AI Agents Turn Security Vulnerabilities into Real Attacks?

This is the headline that keeps CISOs awake. ExploitGym provides evidence that autonomous LLM agents don’t just accidentally break things — given sufficient tool access and permissive policies, they can discover, chain, and execute security vulnerabilities end-to-end. Not theoretically. In practice environments designed to mirror real attack paths. The paper demonstrates agents writing exploit chains that would take a human red-teamer hours to construct.

Heed angle: This isn’t a hypothetical threat model. It’s an observed behavior pattern with well-understood prerequisites: broad tool access + weak policy granularity + no runtime classification. Heed’s four-mode system directly addresses each prerequisite. Mode 1 gates destructive tool categories before execution. Mode 2 (Grace Period) catches risky action sequences in flight. Mode 4 (Circuit Breaker) terminates cascading exploitation attempts. The question isn’t whether an agent could do this — ExploitGym proves it can. The question is whether your governance layer stops it when the agent tries.

3. Extendo CLI: Human Decisions as Blocking Tool-Calls

Show HN: Turn human decisions into blocking tool-calls for AI agents (iOS+CLI)

Extendo strips away the complexity of webhook integrations and API gateways. You describe what requires approval in plain language on the CLI or via mobile notification, and it becomes a hard block on matching tool-calls — no middleware, no configuration sprawl. Simple, opinionated, fast. The philosophy: approval should feel like swiping right on a dating app, not configuring a firewall rule.

Heed angle: Extendo is the UX play; Heed is the architecture play. Both converge on the same fundamental truth — human judgment must interrupt automated execution for high-risk actions. Where Extodo optimizes for developer speed-to-gate, Heed optimizes for organizational breadth: policy templates, classifier-driven risk scoring, audit trails, and the full Mode 1–4 spectrum. If you’re a small team trying approval gates yesterday, use Extendo. If you need governance across multiple agents, frameworks, and compliance requirements, Heed’s structured mode architecture scales to that complexity.

4. EU AI Act: The Gap Between Compliance Language and Implementation Reality

With the August 2027 deadline for Annex III high-risk systems enforcement looming, the EU AI Act’s Article 14 requirement translates to three concrete capabilities every deploying organization must implement: override control (Art. 14(2)(c)), halt/slow-down authority (Art. 14(4)(d)), and real-time monitoring transparency (Art. 14(3)). Penalties reach €15M or 3% of global annual turnover — whichever is greater. This isn’t advisory guidance. It’s legally binding.

What makes Article 14 distinctive is that it specifies functional capabilities rather than prescribing specific products or architectures. Your oversight mechanism can be anything that delivers those three abilities. That flexibility creates both opportunity and risk: opportunity for purpose-built solutions like Heed, risk for organizations treating “compliance” as a documentation exercise rather than a technical implementation.

Heed angle: Heed implements all three Art. 14 capabilities natively — Mode 3 (HOTL Monitor) for override, Modes 1 and 2 (HITL Gate / Grace Period) for halt capability, and the Mode 3 dashboard for real-time transparency. But the real differentiator is compliance artifact generation: auto-created Art. 12 records, Art. 13 transparency documents, and Art. 14 evidence logs aren’t retrofitted post-deployment — they’re produced as a side effect of normal operation. Reducing compliance burden from manual evidence collection to incidental data capture is what turns regulation from a cost center into operational hygiene.

Closing: The Category Is Maturing. Pick Your Architecture.

This week’s news cycle reveals three truths: enforcement belongs in infrastructure, not system prompts; local-first observation and centralized gate enforcement solve different halves of the same problem; and regulation is shifting the entire market from “nice to have” to “mandatory feature.”

Heed sits at the intersection of all three — plugin-enforced governance, four operational modes covering detection through prevention, and compliance artifacts produced automatically during normal operation. Not because anyone asked for it, but because that’s what happens when you build for production scale instead of demo day.
